Heading 1

The house was one of those villas. A large, modern building that probably cost more than most people made in a half their lifetime. Located in a gated neighborhood, it was owned by an eccentric couple who made their fortune collecting oddities. Every hallway was lined with marble busts, abstract bronze shapes, and eerie porcelain figures. Sarah arrived at the house for her babysitting gig. It was the most she had ever been offered for one night of sitting.
"The kids are already fast asleep," the father explained, putting on his coat.
"Once you're ready to rest, you can use the guest bedroom down the hall. Feel free to watch television or use the phone in there."
"We really do appreciate you filling in on such short notice," the mother added, fastening an earring.
"There's leftover pasta in the fridge if you get hungry. Our numbers are on the pad by the kitchen phone, just in case."
The first few hours went perfectly. The house was dead quiet, save for the occasional creak of old wood settling and the distant hum of the refrigerator. Sarah checked on the children once, found them sound asleep, their nightlight casting a soft amber glow across the room, and then decided to head to the designated guest room to watch TV and relax.
When she switched on the bedroom light, she paused. Sitting in the far corner of the room, right beside the window, was a life-sized statue of a clown.
It was incredibly detailed. It wore a baggy, faded yellow jumpsuit with red pom-poms down the front, a fuzzy orange wig, and thick white face paint. What bothered Sarah the most was its expression—it wasn't a happy smile, but a wide, painted-on smirk. Its glass eyes seemed to be staring directly at the bed.
Sarah sighed, shaking her head. Rich people have the strangest taste, she thought, remembering the unsettling bronze sculpture of a headless figure she'd passed in the foyer. At least that one was in a hallway she didn't have to sleep next to.
She turned on the TV and curled up under the covers. But as the minutes ticked by, she couldn't focus on the screen.
Every time she looked away, she felt the distinct, prickly sensation of being watched. She glanced at the corner. The clown hadn't moved, obviously, but in the dim light cast by the television, its shadow stretched aggressively across the ceiling, seeming to lengthen and shift with every flicker of the screen. She tried changing the channel, tried turning up the volume, tried burying herself deeper under the blanket. Nothing worked. At one point she was almost certain the smirk had widened slightly, though she told herself it was just a trick of the shifting light. She even got up once to check on the children again, just to have an excuse to leave the room, lingering in their doorway longer than she needed to before forcing herself to go back.
After an hour of intense discomfort, she couldn't take it anymore. She grabbed the bedside telephone and dialed the parents' number, her fingers trembling slightly as she pressed each digit.
The father answered on the second ring, the loud music of a restaurant humming in the background. "Hello, Sarah? Is everything okay with the kids?"
"Oh, yes, the kids are completely fine, sleeping like angels," Sarah said, keeping her voice low. "I'm just in the guest room watching TV. I'm sorry to bother you with something so silly, but... would it be alright if I went to watch TV in the living room instead? Or could I cover this clown statue with a bedsheet? It's just really creeping me out."
The phone line went dead silent. For a long, terrifying five seconds, Sarah only heard the faint sound of the father's breathing over the receiver, and beneath it, the clatter of restaurant silverware suddenly seeming very far away.
"Sarah," the father said, his voice suddenly completely stripped of warmth, dropped to a dead whisper. "Take the children, run out the front door, and call 911 from the neighbor's yard right now."
Sarah froze. "What? What's wrong?"
"We don't own a clown statue." Sarah's heart stopped. Her mouth went dry, and for a moment she couldn't make herself move at all, the receiver still pressed hard against her ear as though letting go of it might make what she'd just heard more real. She slowly raised her eyes from the floor and looked over at the dark corner of the guest bedroom.
The corner was completely empty.
The phone slipped from her hand and hit the carpet with a muffled thud, the father's voice still tinny and distant, calling her name. Somewhere down the hall, past the marble busts and their blank, staring eyes, she heard the soft, unmistakable creak of a floorboard.
